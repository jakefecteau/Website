About Me

Where I come from, how I got interested in investing, and where I’m headed.

My Story

I grew up in Holbrook, on Long Island. It’s home, and it’s where my interest in investing first took shape.

I attended St. Anthony’s High School in Melville, where I was part of the Leadership Team and a coxswain in varsity crew. As coxswain, my job was to steer the boat and guide the rowers so we moved as one. I learned valuable leadership skills during that time.

Along the way, I’ve worked in retail and event operations and served as an operations lead. From tracking inventory to coordinating projects and improving workflows, those roles gave me a hands-on look at how a business runs day to day.

Today, I’m studying Business Management with a Finance specialization at Stony Brook University, and I expect to graduate in January 2027. Courses like Investment Analysis, Financial Management, and Mergers and Acquisitions have let me explore the subjects behind my interest in the markets.

Why Investing

I started getting interested in investing when I was 16.

The idea of compounded growth really excited me.

Returns can earn returns of their own, and the longer money stays invested, the more time that effect has to build. Starting early suddenly seemed like a real advantage.

My parents first introduced me to compounding. I also went on golfing trips every year with about 50 men in their 50s and 60s. I often asked them for one thing they wished they had done when they were younger. Almost every time, they said they wished they had invested earlier.

Hearing that answer again and again stuck with me. On my 18th birthday, I opened my own brokerage account, turning an interest I’d been building into hands-on experience.

What I’ve Learned

Investing has taught me that time in the market is much more important than trying to time the market. Short-term swings can grab all the attention, but I try to keep my focus on the long term.

I’ve also learned that a diversified portfolio is important for getting through tough times in the market, because it avoids relying too heavily on any single investment.

Just as important is staying consistent. Good investing habits are built over time.

What I Follow

Capital markets interest me, from how companies raise money to how investors decide where to put it. I also follow the tech industry closely, where new developments can quickly change a company’s outlook.

I’ve been following Nvidia very closely for several years. I first invested in 2022, while looking for new companies to invest in. AI was starting to take off, and I realized the data centers behind it ran on Nvidia’s GPUs, so I figured the company could have a lot of growth potential.

In summer 2026, I interned in wealth management at Main Street Financial Group. I entered and verified client portfolio data in YCharts, supported portfolio analysis and research across stocks, ETFs, and mutual funds, and helped prepare reporting for advisor review. It was a chance to see portfolio work up close.

Projects

School · BUS 356 · Excel

Gaming Company Acquisition Analysis

The question: Should a software firm acquire one of four gaming companies or start its own?

What I did: Built out projected cash flows for all five options and compared them using discounted cash flow, net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), and payback period.

Outcome: I recommended acquiring Action Games, which had the highest NPV (about $201 million) and IRR (about 44.6%). It also required the largest initial investment and had the longest payback period, about 5.7 years.

College Earnings Analysis

The question: How do bachelor’s graduates’ earnings differ by school, field of study, and years after graduation?

What I did: Wrote Excel formulas to restructure earnings data, loaded it into a SQL data warehouse, and built Tableau dashboards comparing Stony Brook University, Georgia Tech, and Salem State University.

Outcome: In communication, computer science, mathematics, social sciences, and visual arts, Georgia Tech graduates had higher median earnings than graduates of the other schools with data. Based on earnings, I recommended it as the strongest of the three in these fields.

Energy Stock Screening Model

The question: Which of seven energy stocks was the strongest buy under a rules-based model?

What I did: Compared XOM, OXY, ECA, CNQ, APA, APC, and DVN on liquidity, efficiency, leverage, profitability, and valuation ratios, using the assignment’s scoring rules to turn each result into points.

Outcome: I recommended ECA, which scored highest with 8 points, just ahead of XOM and OXY with 7 each. This was a class exercise, not current investment advice.
